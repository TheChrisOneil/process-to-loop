Auditable Workflows in a Regulated Environment: series overview.

Software features are now cheap to copy. In January, an analyst used an AI coding agent to build a working Salesforce competitor in about three hours [1]. When features cost hours, the advantage moves to how fast a company ships the next improvement. Every software company must now operate as an app factory, and the way to get there is to build the machine that builds your machines.

Speed alone can be rented. The durable advantage is a factory built for one deeply regulated vertical. Andreessen Horowitz reached the same conclusion in its State of Markets II report [10]. It names vertical AI the standout private category and says customers pay for how these products orchestrate their industry's workflows as much as for the model underneath [2]. It names the moat as context, integrations, security, evaluation, and domain workflow [2]. It notes that regulated markets favor founders who can execute factories and certifications, and that many of the best learned at Tesla or SpaceX, where "the factory is the product" [2].

a16z: "Your moat is context, integrations, security, evaluation, and domain workflow" [2].

Healthcare is that vertical. Automating regulated clinical workflows is an active research area, and EverBetter is participating in it. Our research is producing a factory that lets a clinician, a nurse, or a patient create a new workflow in their own space while every safety, security, and HIPAA obligation still holds. That is what makes the problem hard. HIPAA requires audit controls that record and examine system activity [3], a security evaluation after operational changes [4], and six-year retention of compliance documentation [5]. A new clinical workflow is an operational change.

The clinician who uses the workflow carries the liability for its decisions. The Federation of State Medical Boards states that "the physician is ultimately responsible for the use of AI and should be held accountable for any harms" [11]. It adds that "failure to apply human judgement to any output of AI is a violation of a physician's professional duties," and that a physician should be prepared to give a rationale for accepting or rejecting an AI recommendation [11]. Malpractice law points the same way. A physician who relies in good faith on an AI recommendation "may still face liability if the actions the physician takes fall below the standard of care" [12], [13].

Our research is producing a pipeline. A process owner's description goes in. An approved, executable workflow comes out. Every run of that workflow follows the same path. We drive thinking steps out of the process wherever we can, because each thinking step left in is a decision nobody can predict in advance. What remains is highly deterministic. That determinism lets us expose every decision the workflow made and the approved rule behind it, which makes the workflow auditable.

We call the engine a deterministic control loop. Code owns every path through the process. A model is called only at declared thinking steps, and each output must pass a gate before the workflow can progress. That makes every use repeatable and every decision auditable. Anthropic classifies this design as a workflow: models orchestrated "through predefined code paths" [14].

We build that pipeline in layers.

The first layer is process modeling. We apply the concepts of Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN) [6] and Decision Model and Notation (DMN) [7] to workflows that a model drafts. Every step is one of five kinds: coordination, mechanical, thinking, test, or gate. Every decision that can be written as a rule is written as a decision table, with a hit policy the tooling proves. A case that matches no rule is refused; the workflow never invents a default.

Every step is one of five kinds: coordination, mechanical, thinking, test, or gate.

The second layer is determinism. We refactor the business process itself to remove thinking points wherever two competent people would give the same answer. Each unit of work keeps at most one judgment, and the design must name what checks it. Everything else runs as code. The result is predictable, testable, and auditable.

The third layer is accountability. We identify every interaction point where a person touches the process. Gates and checks record who made each decision and on what evidence. Evidence is written by code, never by the model that made the claim. A named clinician or administrator approves the design by signing a cryptographic hash of its exact contents. Because judgment is isolated, the approver knows which decisions, and which liabilities, they are accepting. The ledger holds the rationale the FSMB expects a physician to produce [11].

PHI reaches a model step only under a recorded, current business associate agreement.

The fourth layer is execution. An accepted design compiles into a Gas City formula, a reusable method the orchestrator runs [8]. Every gate becomes an executable script, so downstream work blocks until the check passes. Each run appends to a ledger with a declared retention period.

The fifth layer is regulatory boundary enforcement. Every data source comes from a catalog that declares its data class. A design may narrow a tool's permissions and may never widen them. A source carrying protected health information may feed a model step only when the catalog records a business associate agreement covering that model provider [15], with a current attestation date. A lapsed attestation refuses the design. A different model audits each design for defects the deterministic rules cannot catch.

Each layer encodes knowledge of clinical operations and health regulation. That knowledge is the moat. A competitor can rent the same model in an afternoon. Building the layers, and proving them against real clinical processes, takes domain depth that cannot be rented. Claude supplies the harness. EverBetter supplies the proprietary context: the process models, the rules, the prompts, and the loop.

Our research is also generalizing the engine. We have separated the verification rules from the engine itself. The engine's core rules apply to any process: a named approver, a measurable condition on every gate, evidence written by code, and a signed hash of the approved design. Domain policy lives in a catalog, a versioned file the organization reviews the way it reviews a contract. Our healthcare catalog records where protected health information may go and on what legal basis. A catalog for a bank, a payer, or a manufacturer records its own data classes and obligations, down to the sub-industry. The same engine has already produced and audited designs for financial processes such as invoice matching and the monthly close.

That separation is what turns one factory into a factory of factories. One engine, paired with a verification catalog specific to each industry, produces customized workflows across regulated domains of all kinds.
