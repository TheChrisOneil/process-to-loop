We onboard new business customers, and before an account can trade we have to satisfy
ourselves about who owns it. Around 180 applications a month come through the web form. Each
one arrives with a company registration number, a list of the people the applicant says own
more than twenty-five percent of it, and whatever documents they chose to upload.

Three analysts work the queue. For each application they check that the registration number
exists and is active in the registry, that the company is not in a country on our restricted
list, that every named owner has an identity document that has not expired, and that the
ownership percentages add up to a hundred.

Most applications fail on something small and mechanical. A document photographed at an angle
so the expiry date is unreadable. A percentage list that adds to ninety. A registry lookup
that comes back dissolved. Those go straight back to the applicant with a standard message.

The part that needs a person is different: whether the documents actually evidence the
ownership being claimed. A holding company sits between the applicant and the named owner, or
the registry lists a nominee, or the shareholding was restructured last quarter and the
paperwork describes the old shape. An analyst has to read the chain and decide whether it
hangs together. That is maybe one application in five.

Anything where a named owner is a politically exposed person goes to the Financial Crime
Officer regardless of how clean it looks. So does any application from a company less than six
months old. We once opened an account where the ownership chain terminated in a company that
had been struck off two years earlier, and nobody caught it for eleven months.

Today it takes about six days from submission to a decision, and most of that is waiting.
